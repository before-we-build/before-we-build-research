# A History of Attempts to Solve Human Compatibility

English · [[human-compatibility-history-ru|Русский]] · [[human-compatibility-history-uk|Українська]]

<!-- section:in-90-seconds -->
## In 90 seconds

The same pair may choose each other, live together for many years, and still
struggle to make decisions together—or remain in an unsafe relationship. That
is why a single “compatibility score” is not enough. The first question is:
**what exactly are we trying to understand?**

Across the historical traditions gathered here, this broad problem concealed
different tasks. Some systems decided who was permitted to form a union.
Others described people, arranged introductions, predicted specific outcomes,
or helped change interaction. These lines did not replace one another on a
ladder of progress: custom, typologies, statistics, therapy, platforms, and
machine learning still coexist.

The cautious conclusion for Before We Build (BWB) is simple: name the kind of
relationship, the decision, the specific outcome, and the time frame; separate
what can be negotiated from required conditions; observe real interaction; and
revise conclusions when new evidence appears. **Freely given consent and
safety are threshold conditions, not scores that can be traded for attraction
or efficiency.**

<!-- section:definition-and-scope -->
## Definition and scope

Here, **human compatibility** is a modern umbrella term for different
historical attempts to understand or govern shared life and work. The
traditions themselves did not necessarily see their efforts as answers to one
and the same problem.

The shared undertaking may be an intimate relationship, a household,
friendship, caring for another person, a two-person work partnership, a team,
or a community. Compatibility is not the name of a hidden human essence or a
promise of a universal formula.

The source ledger assembled here is not universal either. It contains more
material about marriage, Europe and North America, institutions with written
records, formal science, workplaces, and commercial platforms. Friendship,
care relationships, disability-centered interdependence, queer relationships,
colonized peoples, and ordinary cooperation receive less coverage.

<!-- section:shared-example -->
## One situation, many different questions

Consider a hypothetical scene. Two partners in a close relationship are
discussing a shared household and a move to another country. One has received a
job offer and must start in eight weeks. The other regularly cares for a
relative, does not yet know whether they will receive a visa, and would lose
their own income after the move.

The question “Are they compatible?” clarifies almost nothing. More useful
questions are:

- Can each person freely say no or slow the decision without putting housing,
  money, documents, the relationship, or safety at risk?
- What future does each person want, and what time frame does each consider
  acceptable?
- Who will take responsibility for the paperwork, income, move, and care of the
  relative?
- How do they gather information, explain disagreements, and revise the plan?
- What can be learned, and which differences or boundaries should not be
  “fixed”?

Before these people met, a platform might have decided whether to show them to
each other. Now they face a shared decision under strain. A successful
introduction, an accurate description, the duration of the relationship, and
the quality of this decision are different outcomes.

This scene is only a shared illustration. It does not demonstrate that BWB is
useful or predict what will happen to a real pair. The same sequence of
questions can be applied to friends, relatives, colleagues, or a team.

<!-- section:recurring-problem-families -->
## Different questions require different tools

At least six tasks recur in the historical record:

1. **Eligibility and obligations.** May or must these people form a union? What
   duties follow?
2. **Description and classification.** Which differences between people should
   be named?
3. **Introduction, choice, and allocation.** Who meets whom, who chooses whom,
   and who is assigned to whom?
4. **Prediction.** What specific result is likely, for whom, and over what time
   frame?
5. **Formation and repair.** What can people learn in order to act together
   more effectively and restore contact?
6. **Governance and safety.** How may power, data, and classifications be used?
   Where is the boundary?

In research, a result specified in advance is called a **target outcome**. It
may be a reply to the first message, whether a relationship remains intact
after a year, the quality of a decision, or safety. These outcomes may be
related, but one cannot substitute for another:

> Permitted union ≠ actual introduction ≠ mutual choice ≠ duration ≠
> quality ≠ safety.

The six tasks describe **what a tool is trying to do**. The four BWB levels
introduced below describe **which area of shared life the framework proposes
to investigate**. There is no one-to-one mapping between the two schemes.

<!-- section:chronological-map -->
## Six parallel historical lines

This is not a ladder from “superstition” to “truth.” The dates mark shifts in
emphasis, not common stages through which every culture passed. Older and newer
approaches continue to coexist.

<!-- section:historical-line-norms-and-obligations -->
### 1. Norms, obligations, and the good—from antiquity to the present

**Question.** Who is permitted to form a union, what do people owe one another,
and what counts as good in a relationship?

**Contribution.** The selected legal, ritual, religious, and philosophical
sources—from the Code of Hammurabi and the Chinese *Yili* to the Quran,
medieval canon law, and Aristotle’s *philia* (friendship and mutual
affection)—make obligations, social context, and consequences for other people
visible.

**Boundary.** These sources do not provide a psychological forecast for a pair
and do not represent every tradition. A formally recognized union may still be
coercive or unequal.

<!-- section:historical-line-readable-types -->
### 2. The person as a readable type—from antiquity to the twentieth century

**Question.** Can stable differences be recognized in advance and used to
understand relationships?

**Contribution.** Humoral temperaments, Ptolemaic astrology, Lavater’s
physiognomy, Jung’s psychological types, and later typologies provided a
vocabulary for proposed stable differences. They also show why clear
categories are so appealing.

**Boundary.** Their historical existence does not validate these
classifications as modern physiology, provide accurate pair prediction, or
establish a natural basis for type. If an explanation can be adjusted to fit
any result, it cannot be tested honestly.

<!-- section:historical-line-measurement-and-coercion -->
### 3. Measurement, prediction, and coercive selection—from the seventeenth to the mid-twentieth century

**Question.** Can regularities be counted, similarity measured, and “success”
predicted in advance?

**Contribution.** Graunt’s demographic counts, Pearson’s correlations between
spouses, Moreno’s sociometry, and the work of Burgess and Cottrell brought
populations, choice networks, and specific outcomes into formal analysis.

**Boundary.** Correlation does not show that similarity caused a good
relationship, and a historical scale does not automatically transfer to
another culture or era. Galton’s eugenic program joined measurement to racial,
class prejudice, prejudice against people with disabilities, state exclusion,
and reproductive coercion. For BWB, it is an anti-model.

For the pair in the example, the first three lines offer norms, type-based
descriptions, and measurable indicators. But their relocation decision unfolds
not in a questionnaire but through interaction. That is where the next line
turns.

<!-- section:historical-line-interaction -->
### 4. From the individual to interaction—from the 1950s to the present

**Question.** What happens between people, and can it change?

**Contribution.** The interpersonal circumplex—a map of interaction organized
around influence and closeness—attachment theory, interdependence theory,
family systems, long-term observation of couples, and therapy shifted some
attention toward mutual influence, stress, roles, time, and repair. Some
research examines not only how people are selected but also how processes
change.

**Boundary.** Some early studies relied on narrow samples or measured only
initial attraction. An average intervention effect does not show which
approach suits a particular pair. Relationship persistence does not
guarantee quality, freedom, or safety. When there are signs of coercive
control, joint assessment or couples therapy may itself be unsafe.

<!-- section:historical-line-allocation-and-platforms -->
### 5. Allocation, teams, and platforms—from 1962 to the present

**Question.** How can people be allocated while accounting for preferences,
roles, resources, and constraints?

**Contribution.** The Gale–Shapley model made the assumptions behind stable
allocation explicit. Here, “stability” means that there are no two participants
who would both prefer each other to their current options. Research on
person–environment fit and team composition added demands and roles; Operation
Match and online dating brought questionnaires, exposure, and mutual responses
to a much larger scale.

**Boundary.** Formal stability is not psychological fit, justice, or safety. A
platform is not a neutral observer either: in an experiment described by
OkCupid itself, the score shown to users changed their initial messaging. This
still tells us nothing about long-term relationships.

<!-- section:historical-line-machine-learning -->
### 6. Machine learning and language models—from 2017 to the present

**Question.** Can a large set of features or the language of an interaction
produce an individualized prediction?

**Contribution.** In 2017 speed-dating studies, more than one hundred features
measured before the meeting did not reliably predict which specific person
someone would be especially attracted to after participants’ general
tendencies were taken into account. In a 2020 analysis of 43 datasets, some
assessments of existing romantic relationships were informative about their
current, self-reported quality; change over time was harder to predict. A 2026
study found modest additional information in language from a conversation that
had already taken place.

**Boundary.** These findings do not establish automatic matching before a
meeting, a causal mechanism, or durable benefits for well-being and safety.
Using large language models (LLMs) to summarize conversations, generate
questions, or support decisions remains a proposed application that must be
evaluated separately.

<!-- section:observations -->
## What the collected material supports

<!-- section:observation-pair-formation -->
### Who meets whom

Observed pair formation may reflect preferences, geography, family, law,
markets, social boundaries, and the people a platform chooses to show.
Statistical similarity between partners is called **homogamy** or
**assortative pair formation**. But group-level similarity does not show that
similarity improved the relationship of any particular people.

<!-- section:observation-within-relationship -->
### What happens inside relationships

Two people’s answers about their shared life are related, so they cannot be
analyzed like questionnaires from independent strangers. Actor–partner models
distinguish the association between a person’s own characteristics and their
response from the association between their partner’s characteristics and that
response. These models do not reveal a causal mechanism on their own: a shared
event, stress, a role, or context may explain the same pattern.

In samples of existing romantic couples, measures of commitment, appreciation,
satisfaction, and conflict helped describe current relationship quality as
reported by the participants themselves. This does not establish causation;
future change was harder to predict, and the findings do not automatically
transfer to other relationships. Therapy research supports a separate, narrower
conclusion: some processes and outcomes can change.

<!-- section:observation-reflexive-measurement -->
### When measurement changes what is measured

If people see a recommendation and act on it, the tool is no longer merely
describing the situation. Platform studies directly show changes in exposure,
mutual responses, and initial messages. Possible effects on expectations,
self-description, and long-term relationships require separate evidence.

<!-- section:hypotheses -->
## Interpretations and research hypotheses

One interpretation of the history is that progress did not lead toward a
universal key, but toward **separating the questions**: introduction from
relationship maintenance, individual characteristics from the interaction of
a particular pair, current state from change, and description from ethical
acceptability.

A second hypothesis is that observing people during a concrete task,
disagreement, strain, and repair may add useful information to a static
questionnaire. This must be tested against an explicitly named, simpler
alternative, for a defined group, situation, outcome, and time frame. A null
result must remain acceptable.

History sets requirements for BWB, but does not validate the model itself. We
do not know whether the four-level architecture produces more useful
conclusions than a good ordinary conversation or a simpler framework. The
recurrence of types, traits, and temporal categories in history does not
validate Socionics, Psychosophy, Temporistics, their proposed latent processes,
or a natural basis for any predisposition.

<!-- section:alternatives -->
## What else might explain the result

Apparent fit or misfit may reflect:

- available introductions and opportunities rather than preference;
- shared class, education, religion, language, or geography rather than a
  special mechanism within the pair;
- power, coercion, discrimination, or economic dependence rather than
  attachment;
- health, disability, trauma, sleep, stress, caregiving, migration, or war;
- learned communication, role expectations, incentives, or task design;
- a temporary state, measurement error, impression management, or a new life
  stage;
- reverse direction of influence: a relationship’s good condition may affect
  responses that are then used as its “predictors”;
- selection and attrition: the intact couples that are studied may differ from
  couples whose relationships have already ended.

These circumstances cannot be dismissed as noise. They can materially change
both the explanation and the practical decision.

<!-- section:harms-and-rejected-paths -->
## Harms caused and paths rejected

Historical description is not endorsement.

<!-- section:harm-coercion-and-ranking -->
### Coercion and the ranking of people

BWB rejects imposed unions and assignments; eugenic selection, sterilization,
segregation, and reproductive coercion; and the use of race, caste, class, sex,
disability, or ancestry to rank human worth and rights.

<!-- section:harm-classifications-and-platforms -->
### Harms of classifications and platforms

BWB also rejects the idea that a type is a person’s unchanging essence and
therefore determines ability, destiny, virtue, or eligibility for love, work,
participation in church, care, and citizenship. It is unacceptable to hide fear or violence
inside an aggregate score, collect data opaquely or derive sensitive
conclusions from indirect data, manipulate rankings, or automatically exclude
people without a legitimate, proportionate, and contestable justification.

Consent and safety are required conditions. Strengths in other areas cannot
compensate for their absence.

<!-- section:non-inferences -->
## What must not be inferred

- Not every culture passed through the same stages.
- Premodern traditions did not form a single whole, and modern measurement is
  not value-neutral.
- A long-lasting relationship may be bad or unsafe.
- Formal fit does not demonstrate mutual understanding or flourishing.
- A successful prediction in one group does not establish a causal link or mean
  that the prediction will work for another group or a particular pair.
- An average that is useful for a group is not necessarily useful to
  individuals.
- The history of typologies does not confirm BWB’s typological mappings.
- The absence of a universal formula does not make careful questions,
  observation, counseling, or decision support useless.

<!-- section:bwb-synthesis -->
## What BWB takes from this history

The following framework is **BWB’s project synthesis, not a result of
historical validation**. It proposes four distinct areas of conversation. The
first sets a required threshold; the other three use typologies only as
revisable hypotheses about narrow latent processes.

In the relocation example, this looks like the following:

1. **Value-moral foundation:** Can each person freely and safely say “no”? What
   commitments, responsibilities, forms of reciprocity, and boundaries already
   exist? This is a normative frame, not a typology or a scale of human worth.
2. **Strategic level / Temporistics:** What future does each person want, what
   counts as timely, and how do they connect the past, present, and possible
   future? In technical terms, BWB links this level to proposed processes of
   abduction—seeking the best explanation, induction—generalizing from
   observations, and deduction—deriving consequences from accepted premises—in
   temporal and existential experience.
3. **Operational level / Psychosophy:** How should paperwork, income,
   decisions, burdens, and care be distributed? Here BWB investigates proposed
   processes of analysis, synthesis, and action organization.
4. **Tactical level / Socionics:** What information does each person notice?
   How do they compress and connect it, use it to draw inferences, and update
   the shared plan? Here BWB proposes a revisable reconstruction of processes
   for selecting, compressing, organizing, inferring from, and updating partial
   information models of one shared reality.

A **hidden, or latent, process** cannot be observed directly: it is only
inferred from speech, decisions, actions, and how they change. These mappings
do not establish an innate mechanism, ability, deficit, destiny, or rule for a
relationship. Culture, law, health, trauma, resources, roles, power, and
current state remain independent explanations.

BWB proposes that AI be designed as a limited support tool. It may organize
information, surface uncertainty, and suggest questions, but its additional
benefit and potential harms still need to be tested. The decision remains with
the people involved. AI must not issue an autonomous verdict, conceal safety
threats, or replace community, professional help, and wise counsel. When
there are signs of coercive control, a joint conversation or assessment may be
unsafe and must not continue automatically.

<!-- section:conversation-questions -->
## Questions to start with

1. Can each person safely refuse, slow, or change the decision without
   threatening their housing, money, documents, work, care responsibilities, or
   reputation?
2. What kind of relationship and what decision are we discussing?
3. What specific outcome matters to us, and over what time frame?
4. What have we observed directly, and what have we only interpreted through a
   profile?
5. What circumstances or power imbalance could create the same pattern?
6. What can be learned or renegotiated, and what remains a boundary?
7. What new evidence would make us change our explanation?
8. Who will bear the cost if the recommendation is wrong?

<!-- section:researcher-route -->
## Technical route for researchers

For each study or system, record:

1. the relationship domain, study population, setting, and historical context
   of power;
2. the construct being measured and the outcome specified in advance;
3. data available before, during, and after interaction;
4. effects attributable to the individual, the partner, the particular pair,
   the network, and institutions;
5. baseline, time frame, uncertainty, calibration, and validation in a new
   sample;
6. subgroup results, missing data, privacy, consent, and possible harms;
7. what the tool does: describes, directs attention, influences interaction, or
   makes a decision with serious consequences;
8. what evidence would falsify the proposed mechanism or show no additional
   value over a simple conversation or baseline model.

Priority gaps include non-Western research in primary languages; friendship
and care relationships; disability-centered interdependence; queer histories
and histories of colonized peoples; non-romantic teams; and long-term
evaluation of AI-supported conversations.
