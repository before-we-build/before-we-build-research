# Human Compatibility History — Source Ledger

Research date: 2026-09-05

## Purpose and boundary

This ledger supports the `human-compatibility-history` wiki triad. It records
sources for a question-led history of attempts to select, allocate, understand,
predict, improve, or govern relationships between people.

The sources do not describe one universal, linear progression. The surviving
record overrepresents marriage, Europe and North America, literate institutions,
formal science, and commercial systems. Cross-cultural variation, informal
friendship, care relationships, disability, queer relationships, colonized
populations, and ordinary cooperation require further dedicated research.

Historical description is not endorsement. In particular, sources concerning
forced marriage, racial restrictions, eugenics, sterilization, or opaque
profiling are included as evidence of harmful attempts to control people, not as
models for Before We Build.

## Cross-cultural, normative, and classificatory traditions

### Louvre — The Code of Hammurabi (circa 1750 BCE)

- URL: https://www.louvre.fr/en/the-code-of-hammurabi
- Type: museum record and translation of a primary legal monument.
- Supports: an early surviving example in which marriage, household status,
  inheritance, obligations, and children's status were governed through law.
- Does not support: interpersonal prediction or endorsement of the code's
  patriarchal, slaveholding, violent, and status-unequal rules.

### Chinese Text Project — *Yili*, The Marriage of an Officer

- URL: https://ctext.org/yili/shi-hun-li
- Type: digital edition of a primary ritual text whose layers and dating are
  historically debated.
- Supports: a documented Chinese ritual sequence involving families,
  intermediaries, names, gifts, divination, ancestors, and an auspicious date.
- Does not support: treating an elite normative text as a complete account of
  lived marriage across China or as evidence of pair outcomes.

### Human Relations Area Files — Marriage and Family

- URL: https://hraf.yale.edu/ehc/summaries/marriage-and-family
- Type: named cross-cultural topical synthesis by Ember, Gonzalez, and
  McCloskey (2021), accessed 2026-09-05.
- Supports: large cross-cultural variation in marriage, family, descent,
  residence, permitted partners, and the role of kinship in social organization.
- Does not support: one origin story for marriage or the claim that customary
  rules predicted private dyadic satisfaction.

### Agey et al. — Arranged Marriage Often Subverts Offspring Mate Choice (2021)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1111/aman.13656
- Type: peer-reviewed HRAF-based ethnographic survey.
- Supports: parent and offspring preferences can diverge in arranged-marriage
  systems; "family choice" and "individual choice" must not be conflated.
- Does not support: treating all arranged marriages as forced or all individual
  choice as free from social pressure.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Aristotle's Ethics

- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/
- Type: scholarly philosophical reference.
- Supports: Aristotle's fourth-century-BCE analysis of *philia* distinguished
  relations centered on advantage, pleasure, and character and asked what makes
  a relationship part of human flourishing.
- Does not support: a psychometric matching system or direct transfer of one
  ancient Greek normative account to every culture or relationship.

### Quran 30:21 — Tranquility, Affection, and Mercy

- URL: https://quran.com/30/21
- Type: Arabic primary religious text with multiple displayed translations in a
  public digital edition, accessed 2026-09-05; translation wording varies.
- Supports: one Islamic normative articulation of tranquility, affection, and
  mercy in spousal life, distinct from a computational pairing rule.
- Does not support: a predictive test, a summary of every Islamic legal or lived
  tradition, or concealment of contested historical gender and power contexts.

### U.S. National Library of Medicine — The Four Humors: An Introduction with Primary Sources

- URL: https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/shakespeare-and-the-four-humors/materials/HS-Four_Humors_Primary_Sources.pdf
- Type: historical teaching collection with primary-source excerpts.
- Supports: humoral classification developed in Greek and Roman medicine and
  influenced European understandings of temperament into the eighteenth century.
- Does not support: humoral theory, four temperaments, or temperament pairing as
  valid modern science.

### Ptolemy — *Tetrabiblos*, Book IV

- URL: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/ptolemy/tetrabiblos/4b%2A.html
- Type: digital translation of a primary astrological text.
- Supports: a direct historical example of comparing nativities to forecast
  marriage, affection, friendship, or enmity.
- Does not support: controlled outcome prediction, reliable birth-time data,
  deterministic inference, or the text's gender stereotypes.

### Lavater — *Physiognomische Fragmente* (1775)

- URL: https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/lavater1775ga?ui_lang=eng
- Type: university-library scan of a primary historical work.
- Supports: the work's existence and a prominent historical program for
  inferring hidden character from faces, bodies, voice, and expression before
  deciding trust or relationship; this ledger does not make a page-specific
  textual exegesis.
- Does not support: valid character assessment or discriminatory filtering based
  on bodily appearance.

### Cambridge University Press — Law, Marriage, and Society in the Later Middle Ages

- URL: https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/77282/excerpt/9780521877282_excerpt.htm
- Type: scholarly historical monograph excerpt.
- Supports: in later-medieval Western canon law, capacity, impediments, and
  exchanged consent structured legal validity; doctrine and actual social
  practice must be distinguished.
- Does not support: a universal Christian, European, or premodern history of
  marriage, or the claim that formal consent eliminated coercion.

### Graunt — *Natural and Political Observations* (1662)

- URL: https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A41827.0001.001?view=toc
- Type: digital copy of the first-edition demographic work.
- Supports: an early shift toward aggregate counts of marriages, births, deaths,
  and population regularities.
- Does not support: forecasting an individual relationship from parish totals;
  the records were incomplete and require strong assumptions.

## Classification, measurement, and coercive population control

### Pearson, Lee, and Bramley-Moore — Assortative Mating in Man (1903)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/2.4.481
- Type: primary historical biometric study.
- Supports: an early quantitative attempt to estimate resemblance between
  spouses using correlations across measured traits.
- Does not support: causal effects of similarity or individual pair forecasts;
  the correlations do not warrant the authors' eugenic conclusions.

### National Human Genome Research Institute — Eugenics and Scientific Racism

- URL: https://www.genome.gov/about-genomics/fact-sheets/Eugenics-and-Scientific-Racism
- Type: authoritative scientific and historical fact sheet.
- Supports: Galton coined `eugenics` in 1883; eugenic programs operationalized
  and reinforced racist, ableist, and class prejudice through the misuse of
  heredity and statistics, while causing forced sterilization,
  institutionalization, exclusion, and other coercive harms.
- Does not support: relabeling eugenic population control as compatibility
  research or treating genetic information as moral worth.

### J. L. Moreno — Who Shall Survive? (1934)

- URL: https://wellcomecollection.org/works/kwjbgxeh
- Type: authoritative library record for a primary historical work.
- Supports: the publication and stated sociometric program of mapping
  interpersonal choices and relations in groups rather than merely summing
  individual traits; the library record is not an outcome evaluation.
- Does not support: the claim that a sociogram by itself predicts durable,
  ethical, or context-independent cooperation.

### Burgess and Cottrell — Predicting Success or Failure in Marriage (1939)

- URL: https://books.google.com/books/about/Predicting_Success_Or_Failure_in_Marriag.html?id=ydwnAAAAYAAJ
- Type: bibliographic record and searchable preview of a primary historical work.
- Supports: the publication and documented scope of an early statistical program
  using premarital and marital schedules, adjustment criteria, and a sample of
  couples to attempt outcome prediction; the preview is not a full reanalysis.
- Does not support: transporting its historical norms, sampling frame, weights,
  or definition of marital success into present decisions.

### Before We Build — Typology Authors Timeline

- Repository source: `wiki/sources/typology-authors-timeline-{en,ru,uk}.md`
- Type: internal, caveated source summary.
- Supports: attributed lineages from Jung (1921) through MBTI, trait traditions,
  Socionics, Psychosophy, and Temporistics.
- Does not support: empirical validation, one-to-one equivalence between systems,
  or pair outcomes inferred from type labels.

## Relational, dyadic, organizational, and allocation science

### Bowlby — The Nature of the Child's Tie to His Mother (1958)

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13610508/
- Type: PubMed record for a foundational paper.
- Supports: the emergence of attachment as a theory of affectional bonds.
- Does not support: turning an attachment label into a fixed identity or an
  automatic verdict about adult partners.

### Hazan and Shaver — Romantic Love Conceptualized as an Attachment Process (1987)

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3572722/
- Type: peer-reviewed primary study.
- Supports: a historically important translation of attachment concepts into
  adult romantic relationships, initially tested with questionnaire studies.
- Does not support: deterministic childhood-to-adult continuity or a complete
  theory of relationship compatibility.

### Leary — Interpersonal Circumplex retrospective (1996; work culminated in 1957)

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16367702/
- Type: author retrospective in a peer-reviewed journal.
- Supports: the interpersonal circumplex organized observable interpersonal
  patterns dimensionally, especially around agency and communion, rather than
  relying only on categories.
- Does not support: reducing all relationships to two dimensions.

### Byrne and Nelson — Attraction as a Linear Function of Proportion of Positive Reinforcements (1965)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/h0022073
- Type: primary experimental study of attitude similarity and initial liking.
- Supports: a historically influential, deliberately narrow demonstration that
  experimentally presented attitude agreement affected reported attraction in
  the studied setting.
- Does not support: durable relationship quality, a general similarity rule,
  complementarity claims, or transport beyond the sample and task.

### Thibaut and Kelley — The Social Psychology of Groups (1959)

- URL: https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Social_Psychology_of_Groups.html?id=KDH5Hc9F2AkC
- Type: bibliographic record for a foundational theoretical work.
- Supports: interdependence theory treats outcomes as jointly shaped by both
  people's possible actions, alternatives, rewards, costs, and dependence.
- Does not support: treating relationships as only economic exchanges or
  optimizing one person's outcome at the other's expense.

### Spanier — Measuring Dyadic Adjustment (1976)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.2307/350547
- Type: primary psychometric instrument-development paper.
- Supports: a historically influential effort to separate and measure named
  dimensions of dyadic adjustment rather than rely on an undefined idea of good
  compatibility.
- Does not support: collapsing safety, persistence, satisfaction, consensus, and
  every relationship domain into one transportable construct.

### Association of Family Psychiatrists — A Short History of Family Psychiatry

- URL: https://www.familypsychiatrists.org/short-history-family-psychiatry
- Type: professional historical overview.
- Supports: family therapy emerged as a distinct systemic field mainly in the
  1950s, shifting some attention from isolated individuals to patterns and
  family context.
- Does not support: early theories that blamed families as a sufficient cause of
  mental illness.

### Gale and Shapley — College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage (1962)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1080/00029890.1962.11989827
- Context URL: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/popular-economicsciences2012.pdf
- Type: primary mathematical paper plus Royal Swedish Academy explanation.
- Supports: deferred acceptance can produce a stable allocation under explicit
  preference-order assumptions.
- Does not support: psychological compatibility, mutual flourishing, safety, or
  relationship durability; `stable` is a formal blocking-pair property.

### Becker — A Theory of Marriage: Part I (1973)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1086/260084
- Type: primary economic theory paper.
- Supports: a historically influential market model of marriage, household
  production, sorting, and allocation under simplifying assumptions.
- Does not support: reducing persons to productivity, treating observed markets
  as just, or treating equilibrium as relational well-being.

### Kalmijn — Intermarriage and Homogamy (1998)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.soc.24.1.395
- Type: peer-reviewed sociological review.
- Supports: endogamy and homogamy are population-level patterns shaped by
  preferences, third parties, and opportunities, especially around ethnicity,
  religion, and socioeconomic status.
- Does not support: the inference that demographic similarity causes good
  interaction for any named pair.

### Gottman and Levenson — Timing of Divorce over Fourteen Years (2000)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2000.00737.x
- Type: prospective longitudinal couple study.
- Supports: observed affective interaction and reported relationship variables
  can carry longitudinal information within a study design.
- Does not support: a universal clinical accuracy claim or causal interpretation
  outside the sample and model.

### Karney and Bradbury — The Longitudinal Course of Marital Quality and Stability (1995)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.118.1.3
- Type: theoretical review of 115 longitudinal studies.
- Supports: the vulnerability-stress-adaptation framework, which separates
  enduring vulnerabilities, stressful events, adaptive processes, marital
  quality, and stability across time.
- Does not support: a fixed pair essence, a single baseline test, or the claim
  that stability is identical to safety or flourishing.

### Shadish et al. — Effects of Behavioral Marital Therapy (1993)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.61.6.992
- Type: meta-analysis of marital and family psychotherapy outcome studies.
- Supports: at least some relationship processes and outcomes can change under
  structured intervention, so partner selection is not the whole historical
  problem.
- Does not support: one treatment for every couple, permanent effects, or
  continuing joint work when coercion or violence makes it unsafe.

### Straus — Measuring Intrafamily Conflict and Violence (1979)

- URL: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ195884
- Type: bibliographic record for the primary Conflict Tactics Scales paper.
- Supports: an influential attempt to measure specific conflict and violent acts
  in families rather than treating relationship persistence or satisfaction as
  sufficient evidence of safety.
- Does not support: inferring coercive control, motive, severity, self-defense,
  fear, or context from act counts alone.

### World Health Organization — Violence Against Women (2026)

- URL: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/violence-against-women
- Type: current authoritative public-health and human-rights synthesis.
- Supports: intimate-partner violence includes physical, sexual, and
  psychological harm, sexual coercion, psychological abuse, and controlling
  behavior; individual, relationship, community, and societal factors interact.
- Does not support: treating a relationship-quality scale as a safety screen or
  recommending joint assessment or therapy where doing so would increase risk.

### Keilholtz and Spencer — Couples Therapy and Intimate Partner Violence (2022)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1037/pri0000176
- Open article: https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/pri-pri0000176.pdf
- Type: peer-reviewed clinical review of assessment and treatment modalities.
- Supports: safety and structured assessment are prerequisites; conjoint couple
  therapy is not recommended for coercive, controlling intimate terrorism.
- Does not support: treating every violent act or relationship as the same, or
  replacing specialist judgment with a profile or generic conversation tool.

### Kim et al. — Generalizability of Gottman Affective Process Models (2007)

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1828692/
- Type: peer-reviewed generalizability study.
- Supports: prominent affective process findings failed to replicate in an
  at-risk community sample, illustrating sample and task dependence.
- Does not support: dismissing all observational couple research.

### Kenny — Reflections on the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model (2018)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1111/pere.12240
- Type: peer-reviewed methodological history.
- Supports: dyadic data require models that distinguish actor and partner effects
  and account for non-independence.
- Does not support: assuming one statistical model matches every causal process.

### van Vianen — Person–Environment Fit: A Review (2018)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032117-104702
- Type: peer-reviewed organizational review.
- Supports: fit research compares needs, supplies, abilities, demands, and values
  in work contexts; empirical support is mixed and method-sensitive.
- Does not support: transferring person–job fit directly to intimate,
  foundational, or moral compatibility.

### Mathieu et al. — Teams in a New Era (2019)

- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520615/
- Type: peer-reviewed team-science review.
- Supports: team research distinguishes inputs, interaction processes, emergent
  states, and outputs rather than equating member composition with performance.
- Does not support: inferring a team outcome from personality composition alone.

## Computer, platform, and machine-learning matching

### Computer History Museum — Operation Match archive (1965–1966)

- URL: https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2020/12/102797880-05-01-acc.pdf
- Type: archival primary materials and historical documentation.
- Supports: early mass computer dating converted questionnaires and preferences
  into mechanically produced introductions.
- Does not support: a claim that computer mediation began in one place or that
  the resulting introductions were validated as durable relationships.

### Finkel et al. — Online Dating: A Critical Analysis (2012)

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173279/
- Full-text URL: https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/PsychologicalScienceinthePublicInterest-2012-Finkel-3-66.pdf
- Type: peer-reviewed multidisciplinary review.
- Supports: online dating expanded access and communication, while public
  evidence available in 2012 did not substantiate proprietary claims that
  questionnaire-based algorithms produced superior romantic outcomes.
- Does not support: saying online introductions never work or that no future
  decision support can add value.

### Rosenfeld, Thomas, and Hausen — Disintermediating Your Friends (2019)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1908630116
- Type: peer-reviewed analysis of a nationally representative U.S. survey.
- Supports: by the 2010s, online meeting had become a major and, for U.S.
  heterosexual couples in the reported period, the most common meeting route.
- Does not support: global generalization or superiority of algorithmic matching
  for relationship quality.

### OkCupid — We Experiment on Human Beings (2014)

- URL: https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/weexperimentonhumanbeings.html
- Type: archived company report about platform experiments, not an independent
  peer-reviewed evaluation.
- Supports: changing the percentage displayed to users changed initial messaging
  and short conversation behavior; a recommendation can intervene in the outcome
  it appears to measure.
- Does not support: the platform's own definition of a good match, durable
  relationship quality, or the ethics of experimenting without meaningful prior
  consent.

### Chen, Hu, and Yang — Reducing Recommendation Inequality via Two-Sided Matching (2023)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1111/iere.12631
- Type: large field experiment on an online-dating platform.
- Supports: changing two-sided ranking can alter mutual-like rates and the
  distribution of attention across users.
- Does not support: treating mutual likes as durable relationships, well-being,
  or safety, or transporting one platform result without replication.

### Joel, Eastwick, and Finkel — Is Romantic Desire Predictable? (2017)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617714580
- Type: machine-learning analysis of two speed-dating studies.
- Supports: many pre-meeting self-report traits and preferences did not predict
  the distinctive desire of one specific person for another after accounting
  for general actor and partner tendencies.
- Does not support: the claim that attraction is random or that post-interaction
  evidence has no predictive value.

### Joel et al. — Robust Predictors of Relationship Quality (2020)

- URL: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117
- Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7431040/
- Type: preregistered machine-learning synthesis of 43 longitudinal dyadic data
  sets from established romantic couples.
- Supports: relationship-specific perceptions such as commitment, appreciation,
  satisfaction, and conflict were among the strongest predictors of reported
  relationship quality at a given time; change remained much harder to predict.
- Does not support: pre-relationship matchmaking, causal claims, universal
  thresholds, or transfer beyond the represented populations and outcomes.

### U.S. Federal Trade Commission — A Look Behind the Screens (2024)

- URL: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Social-Media-6b-Report-9-11-2024.pdf
- Type: regulatory study of platform data practices.
- Supports: algorithms and data analytics may infer sensitive information about
  relationships, income, lifestyle, sexual orientation, or mental health, with
  privacy, stigma, and discrimination risks.
- Does not support: assuming every platform uses the same data practices or that
  regulation alone establishes scientific validity.

### Matz et al. — Large Language Models Can Detect Verbal Indicators of Romantic Attraction (2026)

- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42115286/
- Type: peer-reviewed analysis of transcripts from 964 speed dates.
- Supports: after a conversation, language-model judgments based on retrospective
  transcripts carried modest incremental information about immediate subjective
  and behavioral outcomes in that setting.
- Does not support: pre-meeting partner selection, strong prediction, durable
  relationship quality, causal mechanisms, or autonomous exclusion of people.

## Open gaps

- Non-Western philosophical and religious histories need dedicated scholars and
  primary-language sources rather than token examples.
- Histories of friendship, siblings, caregiving, cofounders, military teams,
  church communities, and disability-centered interdependence remain thin.
- Claims about graphology, blood groups, and other folk matching systems need
  direct historical evidence of pair use, not merely evidence that the
  classification existed. Astrology has direct pair-use evidence here;
  physiognomy is retained only as a broader pre-relationship character-reading
  attempt, not yet as documented systematic partner matching.
- Commercial algorithms rarely disclose enough detail for independent mechanism
  validation; product success, engagement, introductions, attraction,
  satisfaction, safety, and durability must remain separate outcomes.
- AI-mediated conversation and assessment are changing quickly. No evidence can
  justify a context-free universal verdict about a pair; narrower uses still need
  longitudinal, consent-based, independently audited evaluation.
